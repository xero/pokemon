Stage 2 from Haunter. Gives up 3 Prize cards, not 2. Limitless lists it as 2 because their summary applies the generic "Pokémon ex" rule; the printed rule box on a Mega Evolution Pokémon ex reads three. (Bulbapedia))

Unlike the old SWSH-era "M Pokémon-EX", playing a Mega Evolution Pokémon ex does not end your turn. It evolves like any other Stage 2.

"Gengar" and "Mega Gengar ex" are different card names, so four of each is legal. Better still: they both evolve from Haunter. One Gastly→Haunter line feeds two completely different Stage 2 payoffs — the 3-Prize 350 HP monster, and your existing 1-Prize Mind Jack Gengar that gives up zero Prizes under Shadowy Concealment.

That's why Gengar POR 050 stays in the list. It isn't nostalgia; it's the perfect single-prize attacker for this exact shell.

The Real Card Is the Ability, Not the Attack

Void Gale is a fine attack — 230 for two Energy, and it feeds its own Energy back to the Bench. It does not one-shot the format's big threats (Dragapult ex is 320, Gardevoir ex around 310).

Shadowy Concealment is the reason the deck exists. Look at what it does to a board of one-Prize Darkness Pokémon when your opponent is attacking with ex Pokémon:

Your PokémonPrizes normallyPrizes when KO'd by an opposing ex
Gastly, Haunter, Gengar (POR 050), Koffing, Weezing, Toxel, Toxtricity, Munkidori10
Fezandipiti ex, Pecharunt ex21
Mega Gengar ex32

They knock out your Gastly with a Dragapult ex and get nothing. Not one Prize. In a format where nearly every top deck attacks with ex Pokémon, you have deleted an entire Prize from every exchange on the board.

Read the trigger twice: "by an attack from your opponent's Pokémon ex."

Against a deck that attacks with non-ex Pokémon, Shadowy Concealment does absolutely nothing. Fox's decks, single-prize rogue decks, and your own psychic builds all ignore it completely.

This is a metagame card. It is superb against the top of the format and blank against the kitchen table. Know which game you're playing.

The Engine: Toxtricity into Munkidori

The competitive shell isn't built on Gengar's attack at all. It's built on a damage-counter engine you'll recognise immediately.

Toxtricity's drawback is Munkidori's ammunition. Accelerate Energy, take 2 counters as the price, then throw those counters at your opponent. Free Energy and free damage, every single turn, with no attack and no Supporter spent.

If that structure feels familiar: it is exactly Magma Basin from Fox's deck (attach from elsewhere, take 2 counters) welded to Life Shaker from your psychic builds (move counters around) — except the counters go to the opponent.

Problem
Detail
Wrong card name
It names M Gengar-EX, the XY-era card. Mega Gengar ex (PFL 056) is a different card with a different name. The Tool would never trigger.
Solves a rule that no longer exists
In the XY era, Mega Evolving ended your turn — that's the entire problem Spirit Links were printed to fix. Modern Mega Evolution Pokémon ex do not end your turn when played. (Bulbapedia))
Not Standard legal
Phantom Forces is 2014. Rotated many times over.

And it wouldn't help your binder either. You own Gengar V, not Gengar-EX. A V evolves into a VMAX, which also doesn't end your turn — so there's no version of your collection where this card does anything.

The Spirit Link cards were format-defining in their era, and if the modern Megas had kept the turn-ending rule, this would be an auto-include four-of.

How To Play ex Style

This is the part that transfers. Everything below applies to any ex deck, including all three of Fox's.

1. You are not fighting. You are racing to six.

The single biggest mental shift from your casual deck. In Gengar Gang, every Pokémon is worth 1 Prize, so "knock out more stuff than they do" and "win" are the same sentence.

In an ex format they come apart:

Pokémon type
Prizes
Non-ex
1
Pokémon ex / V
2
Mega Evolution Pokémon ex
3

Two Mega knockouts is 6 Prizes. The game can end in two exchanges. You are not playing a war of attrition; you are playing a 2-to-4 turn sprint where each turn is worth a third of the game.

2. Build a Prize map before you start

Before the first attack, ask: which six Prizes am I actually taking?

  • Three knockouts on their 2-Prize attackers — the standard plan.
  • Two knockouts on their Megas — fastest, but requires enough damage.
  • Six knockouts on single-prize Pokémon — you will lose this race. Don't plan it.

Then ask the mirror question: which of my Pokémon can they afford to knock out? If your Mega Gengar ex dying hands them 3 of their 6, then Mega Gengar ex is not a card you casually leave in the Active Spot.

3. The trade is the whole game

Every exchange has a price on both sides. Score it:

You loseThey loseVerdict
1-Prize Pokémontheir 2-Prize exGreat. This is the dream.
2-Prize extheir 2-Prize exEven. Fine.
3-Prize Megatheir 2-Prize exBad. You lost the exchange even though you won the fight.
1-Prize PokémonnothingBad, unless you gained setup

This is why "dark box" decks exist and why Shadowy Concealment is strong. A deck stuffed with 1-Prize attackers that occasionally knocks out a 2-Prize ex wins the race without ever winning a "fight." Add an Ability that drops your 1-Prize bodies to zero, and the maths stops being close.

4. The two-shot problem beats HP every time

350 HP feels unkillable. It isn't, and here's the trap:

If your attacker needs two turns to knock something out, and theirs needs one, you lose — regardless of how much HP you have. They knock out one of yours every turn; you knock out one of theirs every other turn.

Void Gale does 230. Dragapult ex has 320. That is a two-shot, and it is the single biggest problem with this deck. The fix is exactly what the engine does: Munkidori moves 3 counters (30 damage) onto the target every turn for free, and Sinister Surge keeps feeding it. Chip damage isn't a nice extra in ex decks — it's the difference between one-shotting and losing.

5. Gust effects are the most valuable cards in the format

Boss's Orders wins more ex games than any attacker. Because in an ex format your opponent hides the wounded thing on the Bench and presents a fresh one, and the whole game is deciding what you're allowed to knock out.

Casually you can play 2. In a real ex deck you want 3, and you should treat drawing one as the beginning of a plan, not a card you use to fill a turn.

6. Rules that bite harder in ex decks

  • Going first, you cannot attack. With a Stage 2 Mega that's fine — turn one was always setup. Going second and attacking on turn one is a real tempo edge in fast matchups.
  • ACE SPEC: exactly one per deck, total — not one per name. One card, across the whole 60.
  • Rule Box Pokémon can't be discarded to Gwynn. Gwynn says "Pokémon that don't have a Rule Box." Every ex has one. In Gengar Gang all 15 Pokémon were legal fuel; here only the small ones are, which quietly makes Gwynn worse. Budget for that.
  • Shadowy Concealment doesn't stack. Three Mega Gengar in play still reduces by exactly 1.

Same name, same 350 HP, same Stage 2 from Haunter, same Shadowy Concealment, same Void Gale [D][D] 230, same Fighting ×2 Weakness, same Retreat 2, same 3 Prizes, same Regulation I. The only differences are the set symbol, the card number, and the foiling. Phantasmal Flames and Ascended Heroes are both Double Rares illustrated by 5ban Graphics; the promo is a Black Star Promo with a different holo pattern.

Buy the Phantasmal Flames 056. Cheapest by a distance, and 681 listings means you can get all three from one seller without paying separate shipping. The promo costs twice as much for zero gameplay difference.

They share one 4-copy limit, since the limit counts names. Two Phantasmal Flames plus two Ascended Heroes is still four total — so mix freely if that's what's cheap on the day.

Ignore the high-numbered versions. There are Special Illustration Rare prints of this exact card at numbers above the set size (Ascended Heroes 269/217 and similar). Same text, same legality, many times the price — the same trap as the Umbreon ex SIR and the Eevee Poké Ball Pattern. If the number is bigger than the set total, it's the collector version.

Before you buy: decide which game you're buying for.

Shadowy Concealment is a metagame card. It's excellent at a league night full of Dragapult and Gardevoir. It does nothing against Fox, and nothing against a single-prize deck.

If the goal is tournament play, this is the build. If the goal is better games at the kitchen table, your existing dark.csv deck is already legal, already balanced against his, and adding a 3-Prize 350 HP Mega to it would wreck that balance in one card.
